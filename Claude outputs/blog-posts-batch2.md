**Title:**
How Long Does It Take to Learn German from A1 to B2? A Realistic Timeline

**Category:**
Exam Tips

**Tags:**
german timeline, a1 to b2, how long to learn german, cefr levels

**Meta Title:**
How Long Does It Take to Learn German A1 to B2?

**Meta Description:**
A realistic, honest timeline for going from zero German to B2 — how many hours each level actually takes and what changes the estimate.

**Content (copy-paste as plain text):**

```
There's no single honest answer, but here's a realistic range

Ads and course brochures love to promise "fluent in 6 months." The truth is more useful: how fast you move through A1 to B2 depends heavily on how many hours a week you actually put in, whether you're learning in a classroom with speaking practice or just from an app, and what your first language is. Urdu and German don't share much structure, so Pakistani learners usually need more repetition on grammar (cases, verb position) than, say, a Dutch or English speaker would.

Roughly, here's what most language schools and the Goethe-Institut's own course structure point to as commonly-cited ranges — treat these as ballpark figures, not guarantees:

A1 (absolute beginner to basic phrases): roughly 60-100 classroom hours
A2 (elementary): another 100-150 hours on top of A1
B1 (intermediate, independent user): another 150-200 hours on top of A2
B2 (upper intermediate, where most work/study doors open): another 150-200 hours on top of B1

Added up, that's roughly somewhere between 450 and 650 cumulative hours to go from zero to B2 — which, at a realistic pace of 5-6 hours a week (2-3 live classes plus practice), works out to something like 18 months to 2 years for most learners, not the "6 months" that ads promise. Some learners move faster with daily immersion; some take longer, and that's completely normal.

What actually changes your timeline

Consistency matters more than intensity. Three hours a week for a year beats a burst of 10 hours a week for a month followed by two months of nothing — language retention drops off fast without regular touchpoics.

Speaking practice matters more than most learners expect. You can pass a written test with vocabulary lists and grammar drills, but B1/B2 exams (and real conversations in Germany) test speaking and listening just as heavily. Classes with live speaking practice, not just self-study apps, tend to get learners to a usable level faster.

Your target matters. If your goal is a specific exam date (for a visa deadline or Ausbildung application), work backward from that date with your instructor rather than an abstract "hours needed" number — a structured course plan will tell you honestly whether your timeline is realistic or needs adjusting.

The honest bottom line

If someone tells you that you'll be conversational in German in two months with no prior exposure, be skeptical. A steady 18-24 month path to B2, with consistent weekly classes and speaking practice, is a realistic plan that actually gets people through their visa or university language requirement — which is exactly how our A1 through B2 course track is structured, one level building on the last rather than rushing past gaps.
```

## POST 4

**Title:**
telc B2 Medizin: The Medical German Exam Pakistani Doctors and Nurses Need for Germany

**Category:**
Visa Guide

**Tags:**
telc medizin, approbation, doctors germany, nurses germany, medical german

**Meta Title:**
telc B2 Medizin: Medical German Exam for Doctors & Nurses

**Meta Description:**
What is telc B2/C1 Medizin, who needs it, and how does it fit into getting your Approbation or nursing license recognized in Germany?

**Content (copy-paste as plain text):**

```
Why doctors and nurses need a different German exam

If you're a doctor or nurse from Pakistan planning to work in Germany, general German proficiency (like a standard Goethe or telc B2 certificate) usually isn't enough on its own. German medical boards and hospitals also want proof you can handle clinical, patient-facing German specifically — medical terminology, patient consultations, handovers, and documentation. That's what the telc B2·C1 Medizin exam is built to test.

What the exam actually covers

According to telc's own description, the exam tests "the technical language doctors need for their daily work, as well as for communicating with patients and their relatives" — so it's not just vocabulary memorization, it's applied clinical communication: taking a patient history, explaining a diagnosis in plain language, talking to colleagues about a case, and writing clinical notes. It certifies you at both B2 and C1 level on the CEFR scale.

How it fits into Approbation

Approbation is the official medical license that lets a foreign-trained doctor practice in Germany. The exact language requirement is set by each German state's medical board (Ärztekammer), and it typically includes both a general German level (usually B2) and a medical-language exam like telc Medizin or the Fachsprachprüfung (FSP) offered directly by some medical boards. Because requirements vary by state, the honest answer is: confirm the specific requirement with the Ärztekammer of the state you're applying to before assuming telc Medizin alone is sufficient — some states run their own FSP instead of or alongside it.

For nurses

Nursing license recognition (Anerkennung) in Germany generally asks for a strong general German level (often B2) plus, depending on the state and employer, additional occupational German preparation. The exact combination varies more for nurses than for doctors, so again, checking directly with the recognition authority (Anerkennungsstelle) for your target state is the safest step before committing to one exam path.

What to actually do

Get your general German to a strong B2 first — this is the foundation everything else builds on, and it's also what most visa and recognition processes ask for regardless of your specific medical-language exam. From there, add a medical German preparation track (vocabulary, patient scenarios, clinical writing) before attempting telc Medizin or your state's FSP. Trying to jump straight into medical terminology without a solid general B2 foundation usually backfires in the speaking sections, where you need to explain things in plain, natural German, not just recite terms.
```
